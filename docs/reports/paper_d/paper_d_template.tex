\section*{Abstract}
Explanations of machine-learning predictions are consulted most when a prediction is in
doubt, yet explainers are evaluated on averages over all instances. We ask whether
post-hoc explanations are less reliable when the model is wrong. On the UCI Adult Income
dataset, we compare fidelity, stability and faithfulness gap
between correctly and incorrectly classified instances for SHAP, LIME, Anchors and DiCE
across five model families, with the run as the unit of analysis
(<<data:runs_analysed:d>> runs, <<data:analysed_total:int>> explained instances), Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests with Holm correction and a control for the prediction margin; the
Statlog German Credit dataset serves for external validation. Explanations were less
stable on misclassified instances for all four explainers, and the deficit was large for SHAP (median difference
<<rq1:shap.stability.median:s3>>, $d_z =$ <<rq1:shap.stability.dz:s2>>) and DiCE
(<<rq1:dice.stability.median:s3>>, $d_z =$ <<rq1:dice.stability.dz:s2>>). The prediction
margin explained most of the DiCE deficit but not the SHAP deficit, and at equal margin
the features SHAP ranked first carried less of an erroneous prediction. Both SHAP results
held on instances outside the models' training data. Fidelity changed little and in
opposite directions across explainers. The pattern depended on the model family; external
validation reproduced it for Anchors but not for SHAP stability. Quality reported as an average can therefore
overstate how far an explanation of a wrong prediction should be trusted; audits should
report explanation quality separately for correct and incorrect predictions.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} explainable artificial intelligence; post-hoc explanations;
misclassification; explanation reliability; SHAP; LIME.

\section{Introduction}

Post-hoc explainers such as SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, LIME \cite{ribeiro2016why},
Anchors \cite{ribeiro2018anchors} and DiCE \cite{mothilal2020dice} are the usual way to
inspect the predictions of an opaque classifier. Their quality is
measured with proxies: whether the features an explanation ranks first are those that move
the prediction \cite{bhatt2020evaluating,samek2017evaluating,hooker2019benchmark}, and
whether similar inputs receive similar explanations \cite{alvarezmelis2018robustness}.
Benchmarks report these metrics as averages over instances \cite{herrera2026framework}.

An average hides one split that matters: whether the prediction was right. Explanations
are consulted when a decision is contested, when an auditor examines a failure, or when a
user decides whether to accept a recommendation, situations in which the prediction is
disproportionately likely to be wrong. Explanations can raise acceptance of a
recommendation whether or not it is correct \cite{bansal2021whole}, and they can be
unreliable in ways their appearance does not reveal \cite{adebayo2018sanity,slack2020fooling}.
If they are also less faithful or less stable on errors, an explanation can look most
convincing where it deserves the least trust \cite{rudin2019stop}.

The closest line of work relates explanations to model uncertainty. A recent survey of
uncertainty-aware XAI finds that the stability of explanations is reported inconsistently
\cite{lofstrom2026concerning}; uncertainty in inputs and model parameters propagates into
explanation variance, and not all methods propagate it reliably
\cite{chiaburu2026uncertainty}; counterfactual explanations are highly sensitive to model
uncertainty, even when accuracy is high \cite{christodoulou2026impact}; and SHAP can be
used to explain the uncertainty itself rather than the prediction
\cite{cheng2026uncertainty}. These studies vary uncertainty; whether explanation quality differs between a
model's correct and wrong predictions is a separate question.

We test this with a pre-specified re-analysis of an existing benchmark that explained
balanced samples of true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives
for four explainers and five model families, and ask: does explanation quality differ
between correct and misclassified instances (RQ1), does the difference depend on the
model family (RQ2), do false positives and false negatives differ (RQ3), and is the
difference explained by proximity to the decision boundary, where errors tend to lie
(RQ4)?

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Datasets and experimental runs}

Two tabular datasets are used: UCI Adult Income \cite{becker1996adult} (primary) and Statlog
German Credit \cite{hofmann1994statlog} (external validation). The explanation runs come from
a benchmark whose protocol is described in \cite{herrera2026framework}. That study, and a
later analysis of the same runs, compare explainers by their average quality; the present
analysis reports none of their results and compares each explainer with itself, between
correct and misclassified instances of the same run.

Five classifiers were explained: logistic regression, random forest
\cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, a support vector machine and a
multilayer perceptron, all trained once on the seed-42 partition of the data. Each run
crossed one model, one explainer, one seed (42, 123, 456, 789 or 999), which fixed the
partition sampled, and $n = 50$, $100$ or $200$ instances per error quadrant (TP, TN, FP,
FN), so correct and misclassified instances are about equally represented. Of the
<<data:runs_total:d>> runs, <<data:runs_analysed:d>> were analysed (Anchors and DiCE runs
without explanations, and SHAP runs with fewer than ten instances in a group, were
excluded). They contain <<data:analysed_total:int>> explained instances, of which
<<sens:instances_in_train:int>> belong to the training data (Section~\ref{sec:sens});
<<data:unlabelled_total:d>> instances without a quadrant were excluded. On German Credit, random
forest and XGBoost were explained by SHAP and Anchors with three seeds each (<<ext:runs_total:d>> runs, 13 to
37 errors per quadrant). Breast Cancer was excluded in advance, with one to four errors
per quadrant.

\subsection{Quality metrics}

SHAP (TreeExplainer for tree models, KernelExplainer otherwise) and LIME (ten features)
return an attribution vector $e(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{M}$; Anchors is represented as a binary
vector of the features in its rule, and DiCE as $|x - x'|$ for its counterfactual $x'$.
With $f(x)$ the predicted probability of the positive class and masking a feature meaning
replacing it with its training mean, the benchmark \cite{herrera2026framework} computes
per instance the stability \cite{alvarezmelis2018robustness}, the mean cosine similarity of
explanations of $T$ noisy copies of $x$,
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{2}{T(T-1)} \sum_{a<b} \cos\left(e^{(a)}, e^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
the fidelity \cite{bhatt2020evaluating}, the correlation across features between
attribution size and the effect of masking that feature,
\begin{equation}
F = \rho\left( \left\{ |e_j(x)| \right\}_{j=1}^{M},\ \left\{ |f(x) - f(\tilde{x}^{(j)})| \right\}_{j=1}^{M} \right),
\end{equation}
and the faithfulness gap \cite{samek2017evaluating,hooker2019benchmark}, the effect of
masking the $k = 5$ top-ranked features together,
\begin{equation}
G = \left| f(x) - f\left(\tilde{x}^{(\text{top-}k)}\right) \right|.
\end{equation}
Higher is better for all three. Sparsity was a secondary metric and explanation time was
described.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

The analysis plan was committed to the project repository before any result was
computed. Instances are nested in runs, so the run is the unit of inference. For run $r$
and metric $Q$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_r = \bar{Q}_{r,\mathrm{mis}} - \bar{Q}_{r,\mathrm{cor}},
\end{equation}
so a negative $\Delta$ means lower quality on errors. RQ1 uses a two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test per explainer and metric \cite{wilcoxon1945individual}, Holm-corrected
across the 12 tests \cite{holm1979simple} at $\alpha = 0.05$, with $d_z$
\cite{lakens2013calculating}; an effect is practically relevant if also
$|d_z| \geq 0.5$ and $|\text{median}\ \Delta| \geq 0.05$. It was repeated with $\Delta$
averaged over $n$ within model and seed. RQ2 uses Kruskal--Wallis tests across families
\cite{kruskal1952use} and RQ3 the RQ1 test on $\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{FP}} - \bar{Q}_{\mathrm{FN}}$,
both Holm-corrected. For RQ4 the margin $m = |\hat{p} - 0.5|$ was recomputed from the
stored models and, per explainer and metric, we fitted
\begin{equation}
Q_{ir} = \alpha_r + \beta\, \mathrm{mis}_{ir} + \gamma\, m_{ir} + \varepsilon_{ir},
\end{equation}
with run fixed effects and standard errors clustered by run
\cite{cameron2015practitioner}; a difference is explained by the margin if $\beta$ more
than halves and its interval includes zero. German Credit, with six runs per explainer, is
reported descriptively.

Deviations from the plan: (1) for random-forest runs made first, the stored model
reproduces only part of the recorded predictions, so RQ4 uses the
<<rq4:runs_reproducible:d>> of <<rq4:runs_nonempty:d>> runs it reproduces (99\% or more),
including <<rq4:rf.runs_reproducible:d>> of <<rq4:rf.runs_nonempty:d>> random-forest runs;
RQ1--RQ3 are unaffected, as their labels come from the model that ran. (2) For the SVM,
$\hat{p}$ is Platt-scaled \cite{platt1999probabilistic} and disagrees with the predicted
class for <<rq4:svm.proba_pred_disagree:d>> of <<rq4:svm.proba_total:int>> instances,
which were kept. Two post-hoc analyses were added: (3) RQ1 and RQ4 without training
instances, and RQ1 on seed 42 alone; (4) a check of the mechanism proposed for RQ3.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Correct versus misclassified instances (RQ1)}

Stability was lower on misclassified instances for all four explainers
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1}, Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}). The deficit was large for SHAP, in all
<<rq1:shap.stability.neg:d>> runs, and DiCE, in <<rq1:dice.stability.neg:d>> of
<<rq1:dice.stability.n:d>>. For LIME it was consistent (<<rq1:lime.stability.neg:d>> of
<<rq1:lime.stability.n:d>> runs) but small, as LIME's stability on these data is near zero.
For Anchors most runs had stability near zero in both groups, so the median is close to
zero, yet the difference was negative in <<rq1:anchors.stability.neg:d>> of
<<rq1:anchors.stability.n:d>> runs. Fidelity was lower on errors for DiCE and Anchors,
higher for LIME, and not significant for SHAP, whose positive mean comes from a minority of
random-forest and SVM runs while its median is near zero. The faithfulness gap was lower on
errors for SHAP and DiCE only. Of the 12 tests, <<rq1:n_significant:d>> were significant and
<<rq1:n_relevant:d>> practically relevant (SHAP stability, DiCE stability and fidelity); the
model--seed analysis agreed for <<rq1r:n_agree:d>>. Sparsity differences were within
<<rq1:anchors.sparsity.median:u3>> of zero; only Anchors was slower on errors.

training mean plausibly pushes instances towards the majority, negative class, so the gap
is not comparable across predicted classes.

\subsection{The role of the decision boundary (RQ4)}

Errors lay closer to the boundary (median margin
<<rq4:shap.stability.margin_median_mis:u2>> against
<<rq4:shap.stability.margin_median_cor:u2>> in the SHAP runs), and stability rose with
the margin (Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}). With the margin controlled (Table~\ref{tab:rq4}), the
DiCE stability deficit almost vanished: near the boundary a counterfactual needs only a
small change, which varies more under noise. The SHAP stability deficit shrank but
remained, and is visible in every margin bin of Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}. The faithfulness gap,
which near the boundary is inflated because any deletion moves the prediction, became
lower on errors for SHAP, LIME and Anchors. The fidelity differences were largely margin
effects (LIME's advantage fell from <<rq4:lime.fidelity.b_raw:s3>> to
<<rq4:lime.fidelity.b_adj:s3>>; DiCE's deficit reversed).

\label{sec:sens}

Errors were less often training instances than correct predictions (for SHAP,
<<sens:shap.train_share_mis:u2>> against <<sens:shap.train_share_cor:u2>> outside seed 42).
On held-out instances the stability deficit remained significant for all four explainers
(SHAP <<sens:heldout.shap.stability.median:s3>>, DiCE
<<sens:heldout.dice.stability.median:s3>>), and on seed 42 alone, which has no training
instances, for SHAP, LIME and DiCE. The raw faithfulness-gap deficits of SHAP and DiCE
fell to medians close to zero, but the margin-adjusted SHAP deficit remained, unlike those
of LIME and Anchors, and DiCE stability kept a small margin-adjusted residual
(Table~\ref{tab:rq4}).

On German Credit, Anchors reproduced the Adult pattern, with fidelity and faithfulness
gap lower on errors in all six runs (medians <<ext:anchors.fidelity.median:s3>> and
<<ext:anchors.faithfulness_gap.median:s3>>). SHAP did not: its stability was higher on
errors in <<ext:shap.stability.pos:d>> of six runs (<<ext:shap.stability.median:s3>>).
These runs use only tree models, but on Adult the SHAP deficit was also large for XGBoost,
so we know of no reason for the reversal.

\section{Discussion}

The answer to the title question is a qualified yes. Explanations of wrong predictions
were less stable for every explainer, also outside the training data, and markedly so for
SHAP and DiCE. Averages hide this, and would hide it further in deployment, where errors
are rarer than in this balanced sample. The margin control separates two causes. For DiCE
the deficit is mainly geometric: errors lie near the boundary, where explanations are less
stable whether or not the prediction is right, consistent with the sensitivity of
counterfactuals to model uncertainty \cite{christodoulou2026impact}. For SHAP it remains at
equal margin, and there its top features also carry less of an erroneous prediction.

Three consequences follow for practice: explanation quality should be reported
separately for correct and misclassified instances; the stability of an explanation used
to contest or justify a decision should be checked for that instance; and deletion
metrics with a mean baseline should be reported per predicted class.

Limits: one tabular benchmark, with an external check of six runs per explainer that
did not reproduce the SHAP stability deficit; fixed explainer settings, under which LIME's
stability is near zero and the absolute relevance threshold cannot be met; Anchors and DiCE
runs missing not at random (removing units, not biasing within-run contrasts); part of the
random-forest runs in RQ4; a post-hoc treatment of the training overlap; and proxy metrics,
so whether users are misled needs a user study.

\section{Conclusions}

Post-hoc explanations were less reliable when the model was wrong. In
<<data:runs_analysed:d>> benchmark runs, stability was lower on misclassified instances for
SHAP, LIME, Anchors and DiCE, also outside the training data. The DiCE deficit is mostly
explained by the proximity of errors to the boundary; the SHAP deficit is not, and at equal
margin SHAP's top features carry less of an erroneous prediction. Explanation quality
should be reported separately for correct and incorrect predictions, because an average
describes the explanations that are needed least.
