\section*{Abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are used as judges of the quality of explanations, which
assumes that different judges give the same explanation the same score. Three LLM
judges scored <<sum:cases:value:d>> post-hoc
explanations (SHAP, LIME, Anchors, DiCE; three tabular datasets) on seven dimensions with
a 1--5 rubric, in two panels. The second panel also varied the
prompt, repeated every call three times, scored the cases again without metrics or
outcome, and scored <<rsum:cases.shap_lime_nonzero:value:d>> SHAP and LIME explanations
made worse in two known ways. Over all cases the intraclass correlation ICC(1,1) was
moderate at best (highest values
<<sum:orig.icc_max:value:u3>> and <<sum:primary.icc_max:value:u3>>), and up to
<<rsum:between.max:value:pct>>\% of the score variance lay between explanation methods.
Among SHAP and LIME explanations with a non-zero weight the highest value was
<<rel:primary3|shap_lime_nonzero|completeness:icc_1_1:u2>>; among SHAP cases none exceeded
<<rsum:shap.icc_max_any:value:u2>>, because the cases received nearly the same score. When
seven of ten items were removed, every judge lowered completeness and its ICC rose from
<<pci:original|shap_lime_nonzero|completeness:icc_1_1:u2>> to
<<pci:original+truncated|shap_lime_nonzero|completeness:icc_1_1:u2>>. When the weights
were assigned to the wrong features, one judge of three lowered semantic plausibility and
agreement did not rise. With the metrics in the prompt, one judge named a metric in
<<beh:claude|primary1:names_metric_share:pct>>\% of its answers; without them it
scored higher and agreement on audit usefulness fell from
<<rel:primary1|all|audit_usefulness:icc_1_1:u2>> to
<<rel:clean|all|audit_usefulness:icc_1_1:u2>>. Judges of this class agreed on the method
and on the visible form of an explanation, not on whether it was right. A single judge
should not be used as a confirmatory measure, and studies should report agreement
within method.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} explainable artificial intelligence; LLM-as-a-judge;
inter-rater reliability; explanation evaluation; intraclass correlation.

\section{Introduction}

Post-hoc explainers such as SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, LIME \cite{ribeiro2016why},
Anchors \cite{ribeiro2018anchors} and DiCE \cite{mothilal2020dice} are evaluated mostly
with technical proxy metrics: whether the features an explanation ranks first are those
that move the prediction \cite{bhatt2020evaluating}, and whether similar inputs receive
similar explanations \cite{alvarezmelis2018robustness}. These metrics are cheap and
repeatable, but they do not say whether an explanation is clear, plausible or useful to
the person who reads it \cite{doshivelez2017rigorous,jacovi2020faithfulness}. Studies with
people measure that, at a cost that limits their size, and their results do not always
agree with the proxies \cite{bucinca2020proxy,bansal2021whole}.

Large language models (LLMs) used as judges promise a third source of evidence: scores of
meaning at the scale of a proxy metric. In other fields LLM judges show position and
verbosity biases and can agree with each other without being right
\cite{zheng2023judging,gu2024llmjudge}, and their agreement with people depends on the
task \cite{wu2025userperceptions,zhou2025medthink}. Reviews of the evaluation of
explanations organise proxy metrics, benchmarks, expert judgments and user studies
\cite{nauta2023anecdotal,mohseni2021survey,longo2024manifesto}; LLM judges are the newest
source and the least validated. A recent framework rates SHAP, LIME and DiCE explanations
with one LLM judge; it reports correlations among three judges over all methods together
and leaves repeated calls and prompt variants to later work
\cite{hufleischhauer2026xaiarena}. Outside explanations, LLM judges do not repeat their
own scores across runs \cite{haldar2025rating}. In a scoping corpus of <<sum:corpus.size:value:d>> papers
on the evaluation of post-hoc explanations assembled for this work, only
<<sum:corpus.cluster.llm_judge:value:d>> concern LLM judges, one of them a survey.

A judge can stand in for a human rater only if it is first reliable, that is, if another
judge of the same kind gives the same explanation the same score. Reliability is the
weaker requirement and comes before validity: a score that changes with the judge cannot
be valid for a property of the explanation. This paper measures it and answers five
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Do three LLM judges agree on the score of an explanation, on each dimension?
\item Is their agreement about the explanation, or about the method that produced it?
\item How much do the scores depend on the rubric wording, on repetition and on how they
are aggregated?
\item Do the scores follow the technical metrics of the explanation, and what remains
when the judges are not shown them?
\item Do the judges agree inside a method when explanations are made worse in a known way?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Cases}

The cases are <<sum:cases:value:d>> single explanations, each of one prediction of a
binary classifier, from <<sum:cases.distinct_instances:value:d>> distinct instances. They
were drawn from the stored runs of a public benchmark \cite{herrera2026framework}:
<<sum:cases.dataset.adult:value:d>> from Adult \cite{becker1996adult},
<<sum:cases.dataset.german_credit:value:d>> from German Credit \cite{hofmann1994statlog}
and <<sum:cases.dataset.breast_cancer:value:d>> from Breast Cancer Wisconsin
\cite{wolberg1993breast}. They cover <<sum:cases.model_families:value:d>> model families
(logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron) and four
explainers: <<sum:cases.explainer.anchors:value:d>> Anchors,
<<sum:cases.explainer.shap:value:d>> SHAP, <<sum:cases.explainer.lime:value:d>> LIME and
<<sum:cases.explainer.dice:value:d>> DiCE explanations. LIME and DiCE cases come from
Adult only. Sampling was stratified by the outcome of the prediction.

Each case is a record with the dataset, the model family, the explainer, the prediction,
the outcome of the prediction (TP, TN, FP or FN), the explanation as text and the
technical metrics computed for that explanation in the benchmark: fidelity, stability,
sparsity, faithfulness gap and runtime. The benchmark renders every explanation with one
template, a list of ten features with a weight. For SHAP and LIME the list holds the
attributions the method produced, for example ``worst area: $-$0.1336; worst perimeter:
$-$0.0935; \ldots''. In <<rsum:zero.lime:value:w>> LIME records all ten weights are
below 0.00005 and print as zero. For Anchors and DiCE it does not hold the explanation in its own
form: an anchor rule appears as weights of one for the features of the rule and zero for
the others, without thresholds, and a counterfactual appears as the size of the change
of each feature, without direction, new value or new outcome. The
<<rsum:cases.shap_lime:value:d>> SHAP and LIME cases are therefore the main analysis; the
<<rsum:cases.anchors_dice:value:d>> Anchors and DiCE cases are reported beside them as
scores of that rendering.

\subsection{Judges, rubric and conditions}

A judge received the rubric and one case record, and returned an integer from 1 to 5 and
a short rationale for each of seven dimensions: clarity, concision, actionability, audit
usefulness, completeness, semantic plausibility and overall quality. The rubric gives a
one-sentence definition of each dimension and anchors for the scores 1, 3 and 5.

The first panel was GPT-4o mini (OpenAI), Claude 3 Haiku (Anthropic) and Gemini 1.5
Flash (Google), with temperature 0. Its prompt templates and raw responses were not
kept; its aggregate tables were, and they are the source of its values here. The study
was therefore run again on the same cases with a second panel, GPT-5.4 mini, Claude Haiku
4.5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash, also with temperature 0, with templates rebuilt from the
recorded rubric. Its calls were made through the OpenRouter gateway, on 27 September 2026
for the first three conditions below and on 5 October 2026 (UTC) for the fourth and
for the control; Gemini 3.8
Flash returned reasoning tokens in every response and the other two judges in none. The
second run is a second measurement, not a reproduction: the runs also differ in the
templates, the output limit and the cases with three scores
(<<sum:orig.icc_n:value:d>> against <<sum:cases:value:d>>), and the condition and
aggregation behind the first panel's tables are not recorded.

The second panel scored every case under four prompt conditions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Primary}: the rubric above; the true-label field of the record is empty.
Three calls per case and judge.
\item \emph{Label stated}: the same rubric; the true label is given. Three calls.
\item \emph{Alternative rubric}: the same seven dimensions with every definition
reworded, anchors at all five scale points and the dimensions in another order; the true
label is given. Three calls.
\item \emph{Clean}: the rubric of the primary condition word for word; the record has no
technical metrics, no outcome of the prediction and no true label. One call.
\end{itemize}

The first three conditions have a defect found after they were run: the record
contains the technical metrics and the outcome of the prediction, which with the
prediction gives the true label. The clean condition was added to remove both. Of the <<rsum:calls.total:value:int>> calls,
<<rsum:calls.parsed:value:int>> returned valid scores.

A positive control was run last. The
<<rsum:cases.shap_lime_nonzero:value:d>> SHAP and LIME cases with a non-zero weight were
scored once by each judge, in the prompt of the clean condition, in three versions: the
stored text; a \emph{truncated} text with only the three items of largest absolute
weight; and a \emph{shuffled} text in which the ten weights keep their order and the ten
feature names are reassigned so that none keeps its weight. Shuffling leaves the form
intact and makes the content wrong; a judge, which sees no model, can notice it only
from the meaning of the features. The expected effects, lower completeness and lower semantic plausibility, and
the analysis were written down before the run. Of its 711 calls,
<<pcs:calls.parsed:value:d>> returned valid scores.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Agreement among the three judges was measured per dimension with the intraclass
correlation ICC(1,1), the one-way random-effects coefficient for a single rater
\cite{shrout1979intraclass}, with its exact 95\% confidence interval from the $F$
distribution. A dimension is said to have good reliability when the coefficient is 0.75
or more, and moderate reliability from 0.5 to 0.75 \cite{koo2016guideline}. Krippendorff's ordinal alpha
\cite{krippendorff2004reliability}, computed as a check, differed from the ICC by at
most <<sum:orig.alpha_diff_max:value:u2>> in the first panel and
<<sum:primary.alpha_diff_max:value:u3>> in the second.

For the second panel the coefficient of the primary condition is given in three forms:
with the three calls of a judge averaged per case;
for one call, the first; and as ICC(1,$k$), the reliability of the mean of the three
judges. Two-way
coefficients, ICC(2,1) for absolute agreement and ICC(3,1) for consistency, are reported
as a check of the model, and the share of pairs of judges with the same score as a
description. To separate agreement on the explanation from agreement on the method, the
share of the variance of the case-level mean score that lies between explainers was
computed, and the ICC was computed inside each explainer and for the SHAP and LIME cases
together, with and without the zero-weight records. Because a coefficient is low both
when judges disagree and when cases do not differ, the standard deviation of the three
scores of the judges for one case is given beside that of the case-level means.

A pooled view averages the nine scores of a judge for a case. Test-retest agreement is the share of case-and-judge pairs with the same
score in the three calls. The effect of a prompt change is the mean difference in score,
paired by case. For the control that difference has a percentile interval from 4,000
bootstrap resamples of the cases, and the ICC is computed over the stored and the changed
texts together.

The relation between scores and technical metrics was analysed within each explainer with
Spearman correlations between the overall-quality score, averaged over judges, and
fidelity, stability, sparsity, faithfulness gap and runtime, with Holm's correction
\cite{holm1979simple}. The share of responses whose rationales name a metric was counted
per judge. All analyses other than the ICC of the primary condition were planned after
the first three conditions had been run; each was written down with its date before it
was computed, and the clean condition was specified before it was run.

\section{Discussion}

On the first question, agreement over all cases was moderate at best in both panels,
and no dimension reached good agreement. The second question changes what that means.
Most of the agreement was that SHAP records deserve higher scores than Anchors or DiCE records.
Inside a method, where a user would want a judge to tell a better explanation from a
worse one, the judges gave the stored explanations nearly the same score, with or without
the metrics in the prompt. A study that reports one agreement coefficient over
explanations of several methods is reporting, in large part, that its judges can tell
the methods apart. The mean of the three judges was reliable over all cases, so a panel
can rank methods consistently; whether that ranking is right is a question of validity
that these data cannot answer.

The fifth question says how to read the low coefficients inside a method. They are not a
general failure to agree: when most of an explanation was removed, the three judges
lowered completeness alike and the coefficient reached 0.6. They agreed on what shows in
the form of the text, a list that is shorter or has no weight; the stored explanations of
one method share one form. Two of the three did not notice an explanation whose content
was wrong. For these judges and this rendering, a score describes how an explanation
looks more than whether it is faithful.

On the third question, the coefficient moved with the rubric wording, and averaging
repeated calls raised it because the average hides the variation of a judge with itself,
which with temperature 0 was small for one judge and large for another.

On the fourth question, what is put in the prompt becomes part of what is measured: a
judge that is given technical metrics may return them in another form, and then its
score adds no independent evidence about meaning.

The study has limits. It measures agreement among LLM judges and has no human scores, so
it says nothing about whether the judges are right. Both panels are small, low-cost
models, and the conclusions are limited to that class. The templates and responses of
the first panel were lost, so the decomposition by explainer cannot be made for it. Anchors
and DiCE were scored in a rendering that is not their own; their scores, the floor of
actionability and part of the variance between explainers may come from it. The cases
are unbalanced across explainers, datasets and model families, and the groups inside an
explainer are small. A finding about one judge is about one model. The clean condition removes
metrics, outcome and label together, and it and the control have one call per judge. The
control has two changes, and whether the stored explanations of a method differ in
quality remains unknown without a reference.

\section{Conclusions}

Three small LLM judges reached at best moderate agreement on seven dimensions of
explanation quality, in two panels. What agreement there was concerned mainly the method:
up to <<rsum:between.max:value:pct>>\% of the variance of the scores lay between
explainers. Inside a method the judges gave the stored explanations nearly the same
score, with or without technical metrics in the prompt; they agreed when most of an
explanation was removed, and two of three did not notice weights assigned to the wrong
features. Scores changed with the rubric wording, with repetition and with the content
of the prompt, where one judge largely restated the metrics it was shown. A
single LLM judge of this class should not be used as a confirmatory measure of the
quality of an explanation. Studies that use LLM judges should report agreement within
each method, the number of calls per item, the aggregation rule and what the prompt
contained, and should include explanations of known lower quality. The comparison with
human raters and the scoring of rules and counterfactuals in their own form are the next
steps.
